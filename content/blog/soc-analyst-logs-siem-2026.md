## Introduction: The Illusion of the Dashboard

There is a quiet assumption in modern security operations: that a
well-configured SIEM, fed the right data, will surface threats reliably enough
that a junior analyst can triage, escalate, and close tickets without ever
needing to understand what a log actually is. However, veteran analysts know a
hard truth: security does not live in alerts; it lives in raw telemetry. Without
deep log literacy, an SIEM is little more than a high-priced alarm clock.

The dashboard is a filtered, processed, and often sanitised version of reality.
Relying solely on these visualisations creates a dangerous dependency. An
analyst who cannot read raw logs cannot validate what an alert is actually
telling them, cannot determine whether a correlation rule has fired correctly,
and cannot reason about what is missing from the picture. Familiarity with the
tool interface is not the same as understanding the underlying data. Building
that understanding starts well before the SIEM is opened.

## The Core Questions Logs Answer

It is essential that new analysts know what a log actually is, and what it does.
A log is a record generated at a particular moment, by a particular process,
according to a particular configuration. That distinction matters.

Logs are often incomplete and heavily context-dependent. A single log entry
rarely tells the whole story, which is why security analysis is the art of
reconstruction — piecing together these statements to form a coherent narrative
of what actually transpired.

Every meaningful log analysis exercise is anchored to four questions:

- **What happened?**
- **When did it happen?**
- **Who or what performed the action?**
- **What should have happened?**

## Log Structure and Parsing Fundamentals

Most logs share a common set of fields: timestamps, source IP addresses,
destination addresses, user identifiers, process identifiers, event or status
codes, and free-text descriptions. Understanding these fields individually is
straightforward. The difficulty lies in what happens at scale and across
systems.

Normalisation and parsing are persistent challenges for any security team. The
same conceptual event may be recorded differently across a Windows domain
controller, a Linux SSH daemon, a cloud identity provider, and a web
application. Field names differ. Timestamp formats differ. The level of detail
captured differs. Before any meaningful cross-source correlation can occur,
those differences must be resolved — either by the SIEM's parsing engine or by
the analyst's own understanding of what each source is actually recording.

## Source Context is Everything

No single log source tells the full story of any security incident. An analyst
must correlate across the four primary categories that each offer a different,
partial view of the same environment.

- **Authentication logs:** For credential-based attacks.
- **Network logs:** For lateral movement and exfiltration.
- **Endpoint logs:** For malicious process execution.
- **Application logs:** For web-based or SQL injection attempts.

An attacker moving laterally through an environment will leave traces in all
four categories, but rarely enough in any single one to be conclusive on its
own. The authentication logs might show an unusual login; the network logs might
show an unusual connection from that same host; the endpoint logs might show
process activity that doesn't match the expected workload. It is the
intersection of these sources that produces a reliable picture. Analysts who
rely on a single source, or who cannot recognise what each source is and is not
capable of showing them, will consistently miss the connections that matter.

## Noise vs Signal

The overwhelming majority of logs generated by any enterprise environment
reflect normal operations. Detecting activity that varies from the baseline is
what an SIEM is fundamentally designed to do, and it is why attackers attempt to
blend into normal patterns.

An adversary who understands baselining will move slowly, use legitimate
accounts, and follow established traffic patterns. They are not trying to avoid
logs — they are trying to avoid anomalies.

This means an analyst cannot define an anomaly without first understanding what
normal looks like. Baselining is an ongoing discipline of understanding which
accounts log in at which hours, which systems communicate with which peers,
which processes run on which endpoints, and what volumes of activity are typical
across all of these dimensions.

An anomaly is a deviation from a known pattern. Without the pattern, there is no
anomaly — only noise.

## Log Integrity and Blind Spots

Attackers are increasingly adept at covering their tracks. Disabled logging
reduces visibility, and sophisticated threat actors may even tamper with logs to
remove evidence of their presence. If a system is not forwarding logs, the SIEM
does not receive an error — it simply receives silence. New applications, cloud
infrastructure, and IoT devices added to an environment without logging
configuration represent gaps that may not be discovered until they become
relevant to an investigation.

Understanding your organisation's retention policies is also critical, as
evidence may be purged before an investigation is complete.

## Before the SIEM: Manual Competency

Before relying on SIEM, an analyst should be able to read raw logs and identify
suspicious patterns manually. This includes understanding common event IDs and
status codes, and having the ability to trace a user session across disparate
log sources without automated assistance.

An analyst needs to be able to open a raw log file and read it. They need to
understand the event IDs that matter on Windows systems and the equivalent
constructs in Linux, cloud, and network device logs. They need to understand
what HTTP status codes, DNS response codes, and firewall action fields are
actually recording.

Tracing a session across log sources is a foundational investigative skill that
requires understanding what fields link records across sources: session
identifiers, IP addresses, user names, timestamps. Identifying suspicious
patterns in raw logs is a skill that develops through practice with raw data,
not through familiarity with dashboard widgets.

## What an SIEM Does (And Doesn't Do)

An SIEM aggregates log data from across an environment, normalises it into a
common format, applies correlation rules to identify patterns of interest,
generates alerts, and provides storage and search capabilities for historical
investigation. Done well, this is genuinely powerful. Done poorly, it creates a
dangerous illusion of coverage.

An SIEM does not replace reasoning — rather, it surfaces patterns that match
rules someone previously defined. It cannot detect what it has not been
configured to look for because it depends on the quality of its inputs.

## Common Beginner Mistakes

New analysts often fall into the following traps:

**Blind trust in alerts:** An alert tells you that a pattern matched a rule. It
does not tell you whether the rule is correctly written, or if the underlying
data is accurate. Every alert requires validation against the raw data.

**Ignoring parsing errors:** When an SIEM cannot parse a log correctly, it
typically drops the relevant fields silently. The record may still appear in the
system, but the fields required for correlation are absent or malformed. An
analyst who does not check for parsing errors will assume coverage that does not
exist.

**Assuming no alerts means no compromise:** This is perhaps the most dangerous
assumption in security operations. Alerts fire when rules match. If the relevant
logs are not being collected, or if the attacker is operating within the gaps of
existing detection rules, or if logging was disabled before the activity
occurred, the SIEM will be silent. Silence is not safety — it is simply the
absence of a signal you configured yourself to receive.

**Over-reliance on a single source:** Any individual log source is an incomplete
witness. Conclusions drawn from a single source are structurally vulnerable to
both false positives and missed detections.

## Conclusion: Logs First, Tools Later

The most effective SOC analysts are those who master log literacy before they
become dependent on tools. Log literacy is the analytical foundation on which
all detection reasoning rests. An analyst who can read raw logs will use an SIEM
better, write better detection rules, perform better investigations, and be far
less likely to trust a dashboard at the expense of the underlying truth.

Logs are foundational to all detection reasoning; if you understand the logs,
you understand the attack.

**Master the logs first. The tools will follow.**
